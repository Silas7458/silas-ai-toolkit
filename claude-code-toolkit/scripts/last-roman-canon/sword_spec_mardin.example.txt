# MARDIN’S SASANIAN SWORD — THE OBJECT
**Read before any render, prompt, storyboard, caption or scene that shows his sword.** This is the ONE sword Mardin Afzar carries, from the slave caravan at 101 to Badon. It is not a Weyland spatha and never was (00W R-174, 5 Sept 2026). Its dimensions and its steel are the author’s ruling of 6 Sept 2026 (00W R-178). Everything about it is built from THIS page and the reference images beside it in this folder — never from memory, and never from a sentence in some other document. If a picture or a sentence anywhere disagrees with this page, the picture or the sentence is wrong. Written 6 Sept 2026 (S#328) on the author’s instruction that the description of this object is never again lost or misread.

## THE NUMBERS — 00W R-178 [A — author, 6 Sept 2026]
**Overall length: 39 inches.**
**Steel: PULAD — the Persian crucible steel, the pulad version of crucible wootz** (THE STEEL, below).
**Blade: 33 inches** long, **2 inches** wide at the shoulder, **double-edged, with a very slight taper to the point.**
**Handle and pommel together: 6 inches.**
**Scabbard: just over 34 inches** — long enough to cover and protect the 33-inch blade.
The blade length is the same 33 inches as the Weyland spatha (00T R-151) — by the author’s ruling, not borrowed. The two swords are told apart by the hilt, the guard, the steel and the scabbard, never by length.

## THE STEEL — PULAD, THE PERSIAN CRUCIBLE STEEL — 00W R-178 [A; H]
**PULAD** (Middle Persian pōlāwad; Persian pūlād / fūlād; Arabic fūlādh; Russian bulat) is the Persian word for CRUCIBLE STEEL — and the name of the Persian and Khorasan tradition of making it. The class is the one the West later called WOOTZ (an English corruption of the Kannada ukku, the South Indian ingot traded west) and, later still, “Damascus.” The author’s ruling: the blade is the PULAD version of crucible wootz steel — Persian, not Indian; the word Mardin himself would use. [A — R-178; H]
What that means in the metal: a hypereutectoid steel (roughly 1.5 percent carbon) cast as a solid ingot in a sealed clay crucible, then forged at a low, patient heat so the iron carbides gather into bands; etched, the bands show as the watered figure the Persians called JOWHAR, “the jewel.” The figure is IN the steel, not welded into it. The Persian and Central Asian workshops reached the ingot their own ways — at Merv in Khorasan by CO-FUSION, wrought iron melted together with cast iron in the crucible; at Chahak in Iran by carburizing bloomery iron in the crucible — where the South Indian wootz tradition carburized wrought iron with plant matter: one class of steel, regional traditions of making it, which is why the scholarship keeps “pulad” and “wootz” as separate regional labels. The precise Persian term for the watered kind is PŪLĀD-E JOWHARDĀR, “jewelled steel.” [H — Feuerbach et al. 2007, On the origin of the terms wootz, hinduwani and pulad; Feuerbach & Merkel on Merv; Alipour & Rehren on Chahak; al-Kindi on swords (firind = the figure); Verhoeven & Pendray on the banding]
The pattern of THIS blade is the author’s reference image, “MARDIN — the blade steel — pulad-wootz crucible pattern reference”: tight flowing light-and-dark bands curling into whorls and eyes like a fingerprint or a contour map, ladder-like ripples where the bands stack, no straight lines — in the Persian pattern vocabulary a NETTED figure (jowhardār-e moshabbak, woodgrain running both ways) with WAVY passages (mavvāj), of the dark-ground Khorasan family — NOT the stepped ladder (qerq nardebān, “Mohammed’s ladder”), NOT the straight-lined sham stripes, NOT a rose. [H — Khorasani, Persian pattern vocabulary] On the drawn sword it reads as a fine, faint, all-over mottling under the polish, brightest along the median ridge. [A — image]
Historical footing: a late-Sasanian sword from the Dailaman region in the British Museum (WA 135747), sixth to seventh century, is proven crucible steel by metallography; Zosimos in the third century says the Indian crucible process was also used by the Persians; Indian and “Seric” steel is in the trade record from the first century; the excavated crucible workshops (Merv, Akhsiket, Chahak) are ninth century and later. So a fifth-century pulad sword in a Persian-court cavalry engineer’s hand is a plausible elite object, exactly the right place and class. ONE HONEST CAVEAT, recorded so nobody “corrects” the show later: the analysed Sasanian blade is crucible steel but shows NO visible watering (its carbides never organized into bands), and the spectacular etched jowhar is best attested from later centuries — the watered figure on Mardin’s blade is the AUTHOR’S RULING and the show’s choice, and it stands. [H — Lang, Craddock & Simpson, British Museum; Simpson, IAMS 21; Feuerbach, IAMS 25]
What it is NOT: the Weyland spatha’s pattern-welded blade — strips of iron and steel folded, twisted and forge-welded, the serpent-twist core running in the fuller (00T R-151). A pulad blade is forged from ONE crucible ingot: no weld seams, no twist, no layers, no chevrons, no separate welded-on edges. If a render shows a twisted-rope figure down the middle of Mardin’s blade, or stacked laminations, it is the wrong sword and the wrong steel.
For the smith or the art department, in one sentence: “Persian patterned crucible steel — pūlād-e jowhardār — forged from a single high-carbon crucible ingot at a controlled low red heat (below the temperature that would dissolve the carbides; roughly 900 down to 680 degrees C in reconstructions); polished and etched so fine silver-grey cementite bands stand against a dark ground as an organic netted, wavy watered figure; no laminations, no weld seams, no twisted rods, no patterned core.” [H — Verhoeven & Pendray reconstructions]

## THE BLADE
**STRAIGHT.** Double-edged. 33 inches. Two inches wide at the shoulder, with a very slight taper to a spear-like point — the edges run nearly parallel for most of the length and close only near the tip. [A — R-178; image]
**Never curved.** “Curved Sasanian sword” was a straggler in the season documents (flagged 28 Aug, retired 6 Sept 2026); Sasanian cavalry swords were straight and so is his. [A; H]
A raised median ridge runs the length of the blade (visible in the unsheathed reference). [image]
The watered pulad figure (jowhar) covers the whole blade (THE STEEL, above). [A — R-178; image]
Proportion in the reference: the hilt is about one-fifth of the whole sword — 6 inches against 39. [A — R-178; image]

## THE HILT (blade to pommel) — 6 inches, handle and pommel together
**NO CROSS-GUARD.** No quillons — the Sasanian form (00T R-155 amended, 2 Sept 2026: “gold scale-work hilt, no cross-guard”). Where the blade meets the hilt there is only a flat, narrow gold collar the width of the blade’s shoulder; it does not project sideways. Never draw a cross-guard on this sword. [A — ruling; image]
**Gold scale-work in bands:** a scale-work band above the collar; a dark, finely ribbed grip section (dark horn or wood, vertically ribbed); a second scale-work band; and a scale-work **pommel cap** with a scalloped lower edge and a small raised crown at the top. Three gold scale bands over the dark grip sections (character file §9). The scale-work reads gold with bright silver rims in the museum photograph. [image; §9]
The hilt is straight and in line with the blade; there is no pommel knob, only the cap. [image]
**What it is NOT:** not a redwood boat-shaped guard, not a ribbed white bone grip, not a redwood mushroom pommel, not brass collars. Those four things are the WEYLAND SPATHA (00T R-151) and belong to every rider except Ambrosius and Mardin.

## THE SCABBARD — just over 34 inches
**Dark GREEN leather**, straight, just over 34 inches long so the 33-inch blade is fully covered and protected. [A — R-178; image; 00T R-155 amended “green scabbard”]
A gold scale-work **throat** (locket) carrying a P-shaped suspension mount on the outer face; a gold scale-work **mid-band** carrying a second P-shaped mount; a long gold scale-work **chape** covering roughly the lower third and ending in a scalloped scale-work tip. Two suspension loops — the two P-mounts. [image; §9]
The P-mounts are the Sasanian two-point suspension: two slings from the belt, so the scabbard hangs at a slant and the hilt sits forward for a mounted draw. [image; H]
**When the sword is drawn, the scabbard stays on the belt: a green sheath with a gold throat and chape, EMPTY.** It is not a second sword and must never be rendered, captioned or described as one. [A — image, read 6 Sept 2026]

## HOW HE WEARS IT
At the **LEFT hip**, from the studded dark belt with the brass sun-disc buckle, on two slings to the two P-mounts; the **bowcase** with the strung composite bow hangs at the **right** hip. [A — 00T R-155 amended; “Mardin Belt and sword and bowcase”]
Left means the wearer’s own left (00U R-156).
Drawn with the right hand across the body; raised in the right hand in every mounted and standing render. [image]

## THE RULES
1. **ONE sword, always** — from the caravan at 101 to Badon. The built 110 text: “the EASTERN SWORD he carried out of Persia (the only Dragon with no spatha).” 00W R-174: his own Sasanian sword and no Weyland blade, short or long, ever. No sword-pass in either direction: he is permanent core and his seat never falls vacant.
2. **39 inches overall; 33-inch blade, 2 inches wide; 6-inch hilt; scabbard just over 34 inches** (00W R-178). Never resized to the actor or the shot.
3. **Straight, never curved.**
4. **No cross-guard** — never quillons.
5. **PULAD — Persian crucible steel — with the watered jowhar pattern**; “wootz” is the Western name for the class, “Damascus” a later one; never a twisted-core pattern-welded figure.
6. **Gold scale-work** hilt and scabbard fittings; **green** scabbard. No wood, no bone, no brass collars.
7. The at-a-glance test for a wrong render: a redwood boat guard, a ribbed bone grip, a redwood mushroom pommel with a brass button, or a serpent-twist core down the blade in Mardin’s hand — any one of those and the picture is a Weyland spatha, and wrong. (Length will not tell them apart: both blades are 33 inches.)
8. Any note that calls one of his renders “non-canon on the sword” must name the reference image it was compared against. (On 6 Sept 2026 a note that had never been checked against the images sent the author to redo renders that were correct.)

## PROVENANCE
The Persian sword he learned to ride and fight with at the Sasanian court (433–450) — “the long Persian sword and the composite bow” — carried home to Armenia in 450–451, where to his family he looked and sounded Persian (“a Persian sword, Persian military clothing”), and out again to Britannia: the eastern blade the Visual Reference Index approved on 29 July as “quietly foreign at a Roman table, exactly Mardin.” [A — CHARACTER HISTORY; 00P; character file §9]
Weyland never made it and never remakes it. It stands outside the sword-of-the-seat lineage. Pulad was made in Persia and Khorasan and traded from India — never at the Sanctuary’s forge, which welds and folds. [A — 00W R-174, R-178; character file §9]
